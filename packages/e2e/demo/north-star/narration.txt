Today we will understand binary search with a visual example. It uses only three ideas: ordering, comparison, and elimination.

First, we need a sorted list. Here are fifteen numbers. Our goal is to find number thirty-seven.

Instead of checking every position, we look directly at the middle. In this list, the first middle value is thirty-one.

We compare thirty-seven with thirty-one. Because the target is larger, no position on the left can work. We eliminate that half.

Now only the larger numbers remain. We choose the center again, and this time it is forty-seven.

The target is smaller than forty-seven, so we eliminate the right half. With every comparison, the interval becomes twice as small.

The next middle value is thirty-seven. We found it without visiting almost the entire list.

In the code, we start with two boundaries: low at the beginning and high at the end. While low does not pass high, we calculate the middle.

If the middle value equals the target, we return its position. If it is smaller, we move low. Otherwise, we reduce high.

Notice the pattern: fifteen positions became seven, then three, and finally one. That is why the cost grows like the logarithm of the list size.

With one million items, we need about twenty comparisons. But the list must already be sorted before the search.

This is the heart of binary search: look at the middle, compare, and eliminate half. With sorted data, one small decision saves a great deal of work.
